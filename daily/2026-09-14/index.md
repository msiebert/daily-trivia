    question: "What Nepali Sherpa, alongside Edmund Hillary, achieved the first confirmed summit of Mount Everest in 1953?"
    answer: "Tenzing Norgay"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Tenzing Norgay was a Sherpa mountaineer from the Khumbu region of Nepal
      who had already taken part in several earlier Everest expeditions
      before joining the British-organized 1953 team. On the morning of May
      29, 1953, he and Edmund Hillary reached the summit together, becoming
      the first climbers confirmed to stand atop the world's highest peak.
      Norgay never claimed to have stepped onto the summit a moment before
      Hillary, insisting the two arrived "almost together, as a team." He
      spent the rest of his life training future generations of climbers and
      became a national hero in both Nepal and India.
  - id: "2026-09-14-2"
    question: "What New Zealand mountaineer, alongside Tenzing Norgay, achieved the first confirmed summit of Mount Everest in 1953?"
    answer: "Edmund Hillary"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Edmund Hillary was a beekeeper from Auckland, New Zealand, who had
      built up years of Himalayan climbing experience before joining the
      1953 British Everest expedition led by John Hunt. He and Tenzing
      Norgay set out from their final camp before dawn and reached the
      8,849-meter summit at 11:30 a.m. on May 29, 1953. News of the
      achievement reached London just in time to coincide with Queen
      Elizabeth II's coronation, and Hillary was knighted soon after. He
      went on to spend decades building schools and hospitals for Sherpa
      communities in Nepal.
  - id: "2026-09-14-3"
    question: "About how many meters above sea level does Mount Everest stand, making it the highest point on Earth above sea level?"
    answer: "8,849"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Mount Everest sits on the border between Nepal and Tibet in the
      Himalayas, and its official height of 8,849 meters (29,032 feet) was
      confirmed by a joint Chinese-Nepali survey published in 2020, using
      GPS and satellite data to resolve small discrepancies between earlier
      measurements. The mountain is still slowly rising, pushed upward by
      the ongoing collision of the Indian and Eurasian tectonic plates that
      formed the Himalayas millions of years ago. Its extreme altitude
      places its summit in the "death zone," where oxygen levels are too
      low to sustain human life for extended periods.
