    question: "What is the official height, in meters, of Mount Everest, according to the 2020 joint measurement announced by Nepal and China?"
    answer: "8849"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Mount Everest's exact height has long been disputed because of
      differences in snow depth, gravity-based sea-level datums, and
      measurement technique. Nepal traditionally measured only the rock
      summit while China measured the snow cap on top of it, producing two
      slightly different official figures for decades. In December 2020
      the two countries jointly announced a single agreed height of
      8,848.86 meters (29,031.69 feet), resolving the discrepancy. The
      mountain straddles the border between Nepal and the Tibet Autonomous
      Region of China, and is known locally as Sagarmatha and Chomolungma.
  - id: "2026-09-14-2"
    question: "What Nepali Sherpa mountaineer, climbing alongside New Zealander Edmund Hillary, became one of the first two people confirmed to reach the summit of Mount Everest, in 1953?"
    answer: "Tenzing Norgay"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      On May 29, 1953, Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary became the first
      climbers confirmed to reach Everest's summit, as part of a British
      expedition led by John Hunt. Norgay was a Sherpa from Nepal's Khumbu
      region who had already taken part in several earlier attempts on the
      mountain as a high-altitude climber and porter. He and Hillary never
      said publicly which of them set foot on the summit first, treating
      the achievement as a shared one. News of the ascent reached London
      on the morning of Queen Elizabeth II's coronation, June 2, 1953, and
      was celebrated as a symbolic gift to the new monarch.
  - id: "2026-09-14-3"
    question: "What tectonic plate is still colliding with the Eurasian plate today, gradually pushing Mount Everest's summit a few millimeters higher every year?"
    answer: "Indian Plate"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Mount Everest sits within the Himalayas, a range formed as the
      Indian Plate drove into and continues to push beneath the Eurasian
      Plate, a collision that began roughly 50 million years ago. That
      ongoing motion still lifts the mountain by a few millimeters each
      year, even as erosion, snowmelt, and occasional earthquakes wear it
      back down. Because uplift and erosion are both slow but constant,
      Everest's precise height is a moving target rather than a truly
      fixed number. Some geologists suspect a nearby river system is
      speeding up the uplift further by eroding away mass that once
      weighed down the peak.
