  - id: "2026-09-15-1"
    question: "What Polish-French scientist became the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields, physics and chemistry?"
    answer: "Marie Curie"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Marie Curie was born Maria Sklodowska in Warsaw in 1867 and moved to
      Paris to study physics and mathematics at the Sorbonne, since Polish
      universities of the era barred women. She shared the 1903 Nobel Prize
      in Physics for her pioneering research into radioactivity — a term she
      herself coined — and then won the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone
      for isolating pure radium and polonium. She remains the only person
      ever to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences, and her family's
      total of five Nobel Prizes across two generations is unmatched by any
      other family.
  - id: "2026-09-15-2"
    question: "What glowing element did Marie Curie discover in 1898 alongside her husband Pierre, work that later won her a share of the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics?"
    answer: "Radium"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Working out of a converted shed at the Paris School of Physics, Marie
      and Pierre Curie spent years processing tons of a uranium ore called
      pitchblende by hand to isolate radium in usable quantities. The
      element's intense radioactivity causes it to glow faintly blue in the
      dark, a property that made it a scientific sensation and, later, a
      dangerously popular ingredient in consumer products like glow-in-the-
      dark watch dials. Radium's medical use in early cancer treatment grew
      directly out of the Curies' research, even as its health risks were
      not yet understood.
  - id: "2026-09-15-3"
    question: "What blood disorder, caused by decades of unshielded exposure to radioactive materials, killed Marie Curie in 1934?"
    answer: "Aplastic anemia"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Marie Curie worked for decades with radioactive materials before their
      dangers were understood, carrying test tubes of radium in her pockets
      and storing samples in her desk drawer, and she also operated mobile
      X-ray units near the front lines during World War I. The cumulative
      radiation exposure damaged her bone marrow over time, leading to
      aplastic anemia, a condition in which the body stops producing enough
      new blood cells. She died from it in 1934 at age 66. Decades later,
      her papers and notebooks remain so radioactive that they are kept in
      lead-lined boxes and require protective clothing to handle.
