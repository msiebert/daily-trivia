    question: "About how many complete and partial copies of the Gutenberg Bible, printed around 1455, are documented to survive today out of the roughly 180 originally produced?"
    answer: "49"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      Only around 180 copies of the Gutenberg Bible were ever printed,
      split between roughly 150 paper copies and 30 pricier copies made
      on vellum, or calfskin parchment, for wealthier buyers. Today only
      49 copies are documented to survive in any form, and just 21 of
      those are complete. Each surviving copy is now one of the most
      valuable printed books in the world, with libraries and museums
      fiercely guarding the ones they hold. Their scarcity has also made
      them essential to scholars studying the earliest years of European
      printing.
  - id: "2026-09-16-2"
    question: "What existing mechanical device, borrowed from an unrelated craft and adapted to apply firm, even pressure onto paper or vellum, formed the basis of Gutenberg's printing press design?"
    answer: "Wine press"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      Gutenberg needed a way to press paper evenly and firmly against
      inked metal type, and rather than invent an entirely new mechanism,
      he adapted a screw-based device already common in winemaking,
      olive-oil pressing, and papermaking. Turning a large wooden screw
      lowered a flat plate onto the type bed with steady, even force,
      exactly what clean, legible printing required. This borrowed
      mechanical design remained the basic model for printing presses for
      the next 350 years, largely unchanged until the Industrial
      Revolution introduced iron and steam power. It's a good example of
      Gutenberg's real genius being less about one brand-new machine than
      about combining existing technologies into a working system.
  - id: "2026-09-16-3"
    question: "What trade had Johannes Gutenberg trained in before applying its metalworking techniques to casting durable, reusable printing type?"
    answer: "Goldsmith"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      Before turning to printing, Gutenberg trained and worked in his
      native Mainz, Germany in a craft built on metallurgy, casting, and
      fine engraving. That background proved essential: he drew on it to
      develop a fast-casting lead alloy for individual, reusable letter
      blocks, and to engrave the precise metal punches used to mold them.
      Without that specific hands-on skill, the durable, uniform movable
      type his whole system depended on likely wouldn't have been
      possible. It's a reminder that Gutenberg's breakthrough was as much
      a metalworking achievement as a mechanical one.
