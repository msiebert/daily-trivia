  - id: "2026-09-19-1"
    question: "What volcano on Mars, roughly three times the height of Mount Everest, holds the record as the tallest known volcano in the solar system?"
    answer: "Olympus Mons"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Olympus Mons rises about 13.6 miles (22 km) above the surrounding
      plains, making it nearly three times the height of Mount Everest,
      and it spans roughly 374 miles (600 km) across -- an area comparable
      to the state of Arizona. It's a shield volcano, built up gradually by
      countless slow lava flows rather than explosive eruptions. Because
      Mars has no moving tectonic plates to carry a volcanic hot spot away
      from its magma source, as happens on Earth, Olympus Mons was free to
      keep growing in the same spot for hundreds of millions of years.
      Mars's atmosphere is so thin that its summit effectively pokes out
      above it, into the edge of space.
  - id: "2026-09-19-2"
    question: "What vast system of canyons on Mars, stretching roughly 2,500 miles -- about a fifth of the way around the planet -- dwarfs Earth's Grand Canyon in scale?"
    answer: "Valles Marineris"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Valles Marineris runs for about 2,500 miles (4,000 km) along the
      Martian equator and plunges as deep as 6 miles (10 km), making it far
      longer, wider, and deeper than Earth's Grand Canyon -- if it were laid
      across the United States, it would stretch coast to coast. Unlike the
      Grand Canyon, it wasn't primarily carved by flowing water; most
      scientists think it formed as the crust cracked and pulled apart
      while the nearby Tharsis volcanic region bulged upward. It's named
      for NASA's Mariner 9 spacecraft, which discovered it in 1971 during
      the first successful mission to orbit another planet.
  - id: "2026-09-19-3"
    question: "Mars's atmosphere, at less than 1% the pressure of Earth's, is composed almost entirely of what gas?"
    answer: "Carbon dioxide"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      About 95% of Mars's thin atmosphere is carbon dioxide, with small
      amounts of nitrogen and argon making up most of the rest. The
      atmosphere is so thin -- averaging roughly 1% of Earth's surface
      pressure -- that liquid water can't stay stable on the surface for
      long, quickly freezing or boiling away depending on the temperature.
      That thinness also leaves Mars with little shielding from solar and
      cosmic radiation and drives wide daily temperature swings. Scientists
      think Mars once held a thicker atmosphere before much of it was
      stripped away by the solar wind over billions of years, since the
      planet lacks the global magnetic field that protects Earth's air.
