  - id: "2026-09-20-1"
    question: "What term describes the small group of mammals, including the platypus, that lay eggs instead of giving live birth?"
    answer: "Monotremes"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Monotremes are the only order of mammals that reproduce by laying
      eggs, and today there are just five living species: the platypus and
      four kinds of echidna, all native to Australia and New Guinea.
      Despite laying eggs, monotreme mothers still nurse their young with
      milk -- but since they lack nipples, they secrete it through pores in
      the skin that the hatchlings lap up. A female platypus typically lays
      one to three small, leathery eggs and incubates them curled around
      her body for about ten days before they hatch.
  - id: "2026-09-20-2"
    question: "Male platypuses have a spur on each hind leg that delivers a substance so painful ordinary painkillers barely touch it -- what is this substance, one of the very few produced by any mammal?"
    answer: "Venom"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Each male platypus carries a sharp, hollow spur on its hind ankle
      connected to a gland that produces venom, making it one of the only
      venomous mammals on Earth. It's rarely fatal to humans, but the
      venom causes intense swelling and pain that can last for days and
      resists ordinary painkillers. Venom production ramps up during
      breeding season, which suggests the spurs evolved mainly as a
      weapon for males competing over mates rather than for defense
      against predators.
  - id: "2026-09-20-3"
    question: "What sixth sense lets a platypus hunt in complete darkness underwater, using about 40,000 receptors packed into its bill to pick up the nerve signals given off by prey's muscle contractions?"
    answer: "Electroreception"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      A platypus closes its eyes, ears, and nostrils completely while
      foraging underwater, relying instead on electroreception to find
      food. Roughly 40,000 receptors packed into its bill detect the tiny
      signals given off by the muscle contractions of shrimp, insect
      larvae, and other prey hiding in mud or gravel, and the platypus
      sweeps its head side to side to pinpoint the source. Sharks and rays
      use a similar sense underwater, but the platypus is one of the very
      few mammals known to have it.
