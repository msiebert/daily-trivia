    question: "GPS satellites carry atomic clocks that, left uncorrected, would drift ahead of ground clocks by roughly how many microseconds each day -- a mismatch predicted by Einstein's theories of relativity that engineers must correct for accurate positioning?"
    answer: "38"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      GPS satellites orbit about 12,500 miles up, moving fast and sitting in
      much weaker gravity than clocks on the ground -- two conditions that
      relativity says will change how time passes for them. General
      relativity makes their clocks run about 45 microseconds a day faster
      because gravity is weaker up there, while special relativity, driven
      by their orbital speed, slows those same clocks by about 7
      microseconds a day. The net effect is roughly 38 microseconds of daily
      gain, which sounds tiny but would translate into position errors of
      several miles within a single day if left alone. Engineers solved this
      before launch by deliberately tuning each satellite's clock rate
      slightly slow, so relativity brings it back in step with clocks on
      Earth.
  - id: "2026-09-27-2"
    question: "The nominal GPS satellite constellation is arranged in how many orbital planes circling the Earth, each holding four satellites tilted 55 degrees from the equator?"
    answer: "6"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      The GPS design calls for 24 satellites split evenly across six orbital
      planes, four satellites per plane, each plane tilted 55 degrees
      relative to the equator. Flying about 12,500 miles above Earth in
      medium Earth orbit, each satellite circles the planet twice a day,
      and the six-plane layout is spaced so that a receiver anywhere on
      Earth's surface can typically see at least four satellites at once --
      the minimum needed to calculate a 3D position and correct for the
      receiver's own clock error. In practice the U.S. Space Force keeps
      more than 30 satellites in orbit at any time, with the extras
      providing redundancy and improved coverage beyond the original
      24-satellite baseline.
  - id: "2026-09-27-3"
    question: "In what year did the U.S. government switch off 'Selective Availability,' the deliberate signal degradation that had limited civilian GPS accuracy to about 100 meters, instantly making everyday GPS receivers roughly ten times more precise?"
    answer: "2000"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      For years after GPS opened to civilian use, the U.S. military
      intentionally scrambled the public signal through a policy called
      Selective Availability, keeping ordinary receivers accurate to only
      about 100 meters while the military used a separate, precise decoded
      signal. President Bill Clinton ordered the practice discontinued
      effective midnight on May 1-2, 2000, and civilian accuracy improved
      almost overnight to roughly 10 to 20 meters. That single policy change
      is widely credited with kicking off the boom in consumer GPS devices,
      from car navigation units to handheld hiking receivers, and it paved
      the way for the location services built into modern smartphones.
