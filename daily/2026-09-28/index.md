  - id: "2026-09-28-1"
    question: "In L. Frank Baum's original 1900 novel, what color were Dorothy's magic slippers before MGM changed them for the 1939 film The Wizard of Oz?"
    answer: "Silver"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      In Baum's book The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, Dorothy's enchanted
      footwear is silver, not ruby. When MGM adapted the story for the
      screen in 1939, filmmakers switched the color to a sparkling ruby red
      specifically to take advantage of the new three-strip Technicolor
      process -- red popped dramatically against the yellow brick road in a
      way silver never would have. The film's ruby slippers became so
      iconic that they overshadowed the book's original version in popular
      memory, and one surviving pair from the production later sold at
      auction for millions of dollars.
  - id: "2026-09-28-2"
    question: "What ballad from The Wizard of Oz was nearly cut from the finished 1939 film by studio executives who found it slow, before it went on to win the Academy Award for Best Original Song?"
    answer: "Over the Rainbow"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      Studio executives at MGM, including production chief Louis B. Mayer,
      worried the wistful farm-set ballad slowed the movie down and
      pushed to cut it after early screenings. Songwriters Harold Arlen and
      Yip Harburg, along with associate producer Arthur Freed, fought to
      keep it in, and the song went on to win the 1940 Academy Award for
      Best Original Song. It later became Judy Garland's signature song for
      the rest of her career and is frequently cited among the greatest
      film songs ever written.
  - id: "2026-09-28-3"
    question: "What color film process did The Wizard of Oz showcase in 1939 to turn Dorothy's arrival in Oz from black-and-white into vivid color on screen?"
    answer: "Technicolor"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      The film opens in sepia-toned black and white for the Kansas scenes,
      then bursts into full three-strip color the moment Dorothy steps out
      of her farmhouse into Oz -- one of cinema's most famous visual
      transitions. Three-strip Technicolor was still an expensive, cutting-
      edge process in 1939, requiring bulky cameras and enormous banks of
      lighting on set. The contrast between drab Kansas and dazzling Oz was
      a deliberate storytelling choice, using the technology itself to
      signal that Dorothy had left the ordinary world behind.
