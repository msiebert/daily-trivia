    question: "What animal's head did Shiva use to replace Ganesha's own after striking it off in anger, according to the most popular version of the myth?"
    answer: "Elephant"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      In the most widely told version of the story, Parvati created a boy
      from clay to guard her privacy while bathing, without Shiva's
      knowledge. When Shiva returned and the boy refused to let him pass, a
      furious Shiva struck off his head. To console the grieving Parvati,
      Shiva sent his attendants to bring back the head of the first
      creature they found, which turned out to be an elephant, and used it
      to restore the boy to life as Ganesha. The resulting elephant-headed
      form became one of the most recognizable images in Hindu art and
      devotion.
  - id: "2026-09-29-2"
    question: "Ganesha is said to have broken off one of his own tusks to use as a makeshift pen so he could keep transcribing which epic Sanskrit poem, dictated nonstop by the sage Vyasa?"
    answer: "Mahabharata"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      According to tradition, the sage Vyasa needed a scribe who could keep
      pace with his uninterrupted recitation of the Mahabharata, one of the
      longest epic poems in world literature. Ganesha agreed on the
      condition that Vyasa never pause, while Vyasa insisted Ganesha
      understand every verse before writing it down, giving himself
      occasional breathing room by composing especially dense passages.
      Partway through, Ganesha's writing implement broke, so he snapped off
      one of his own tusks rather than stop. It's one of several myths
      offered to explain why he is usually shown with only one full tusk.
  - id: "2026-09-29-3"
    question: "What tiny creature serves as Ganesha's vahana, or mount, traditionally read as a symbol of the small desires and obstacles he has tamed?"
    answer: "Mouse"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Ganesha is traditionally depicted riding or accompanied by a small
      mouse, known in Sanskrit as Mushika. Despite Ganesha's great bulk,
      the mouse is said to carry him with ease, a deliberate paradox meant
      to show that spiritual power has nothing to do with physical size.
      Interpretations vary, describing the mouse as a stand-in for desire,
      ego, or the minor obstacles that can derail any undertaking --
      forces Ganesha, remover of obstacles, has mastered rather than being
      ruled by. The pairing appears throughout South and Southeast Asian
      temple carvings and festival idols.
