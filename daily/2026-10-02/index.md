  - id: "2026-10-02-1"
    question: "What ancient civilization's royal tombs have yielded jars of honey still perfectly edible thousands of years later, thanks to the stuff's natural resistance to spoiling?"
    answer: "Egypt"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Honey almost never spoils because of a combination of traits bees
      bake into it: it holds very little water, it's acidic enough to be
      inhospitable to most bacteria and mold, and it's hygroscopic, meaning
      it pulls moisture out of any microbe that tries to grow in it,
      drying it out before it can take hold. Archaeologists excavating
      ancient Egyptian tombs, including Tutankhamun's, have found sealed
      pots of honey thousands of years old that were reportedly still
      edible. Honey found at other ancient sites around the world has been
      dated back even further, well over 5,000 years, making it one of the
      only foods that can last essentially indefinitely if kept sealed and
      dry.
  - id: "2026-10-02-2"
    question: "What dance do honeybee scouts perform inside the hive to tell nestmates the direction and distance to a good patch of flowers?"
    answer: "Waggle dance"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      A returning forager bee runs through a figure-eight pattern on the
      honeycomb, vibrating her body rapidly during the straight middle run
      of the figure eight. The angle of that straight run relative to
      straight up on the comb encodes the direction to the flowers relative
      to the sun, while the length of time she waggles encodes the
      distance. Nestmates crowd around to follow along and then fly off
      using that encoded information to find the same flowers. It's one of
      the few known non-human communication systems that conveys abstract,
      symbolic information about a location the dancer isn't currently
      pointing at.
  - id: "2026-10-02-3"
    question: "About how many individual flowers must a colony's foraging honeybees visit to gather enough nectar for a single pound of honey?"
    answer: "2 million"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      A single bee only produces a tiny fraction of a teaspoon of honey in
      her lifetime, so it takes the combined effort of an entire colony to
      add up to anything substantial. Collecting enough nectar for just one
      pound of finished honey takes roughly two million flower visits, and
      the foragers making those visits fly a combined distance of around
      55,000 miles in the process -- more than twice around the Earth. Once
      the nectar is back at the hive, bees pass it mouth to mouth and fan
      it with their wings to evaporate most of its water content before
      sealing it into wax cells as honey.
