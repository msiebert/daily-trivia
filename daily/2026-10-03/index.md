  - id: "2026-10-03-1"
    question: "What stream of electrically charged particles, constantly blown outward from the Sun, collides with Earth's upper atmosphere to spark the shimmering curtains of the aurora borealis?"
    answer: "Solar wind"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      The Sun constantly streams charged particles outward in what's
      called the solar wind, and when that stream reaches Earth, the
      planet's magnetic field deflects most of it but funnels some down
      near the poles. There, the particles slam into oxygen and nitrogen
      atoms in the upper atmosphere, exciting them with extra energy. As
      each atom relaxes back to its normal state a fraction of a second
      later, it releases that energy as a photon of light -- millions of
      these tiny flashes together produce the shimmering curtains people
      see overhead. Because the particles get guided along magnetic field
      lines, this show is confined to a ring-shaped zone around each pole
      called the auroral oval, which is why the lights are rarely seen far
      from high latitudes except during major solar storms.
  - id: "2026-10-03-2"
    question: "What atmospheric gas, when struck by incoming particles roughly 60 to 190 miles up, produces the vivid green that is the aurora borealis's most common color?"
    answer: "Oxygen"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      The aurora's exact color depends on which gas gets excited and how
      high up the collision happens. Oxygen is responsible for both of
      the aurora's most iconic colors: around 60 to 190 miles up it glows
      green, the color seen in the vast majority of displays, while
      higher still -- above about 180 miles -- the thinner air makes
      oxygen glow red instead. Nitrogen, excited lower in the atmosphere,
      tends to add blue and purple fringes along the bottom edges of a
      display. Because green oxygen emissions happen at the altitude
      where the most collisions occur, green ends up being the aurora's
      default color almost everywhere it's seen.
  - id: "2026-10-03-3"
    question: "What 17th-century Italian astronomer is credited with coining the term 'aurora borealis' after watching a glowing night sky over northern Italy in 1619, naming it for the Roman goddess of dawn and the Greek god of the north wind?"
    answer: "Galileo"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      In 1619, Galileo Galilei watched a glowing night sky over northern
      Italy and, struck by its resemblance to a sunrise, borrowed the
      name of Aurora, the Roman goddess of dawn. He paired it with
      "borealis," from Boreas, the Greek god of the north wind, giving
      the phenomenon the scientific name still used today: aurora
      borealis. The matching display around the South Pole is called the
      aurora australis, named for the Latin word for "southern." Galileo's
      naming predates any real understanding of the physics behind the
      lights by nearly three centuries -- the link to solar particles and
      Earth's magnetic field wasn't worked out until the early 1900s.
