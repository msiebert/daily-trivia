  - id: "2026-10-04-1"
    question: "Which French scholar announced in 1822 that he had finally cracked the code of Egyptian hieroglyphs by comparing scripts on the Rosetta Stone?"
    answer: "Jean-Francois Champollion"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      Champollion had spent over a decade obsessed with the problem, drawing on his knowledge of
      Coptic and earlier work by English polymath Thomas Young. By matching royal names like
      Ptolemy and Cleopatra across the stone's Greek and hieroglyphic text, he realized hieroglyphs
      were not purely symbolic but included phonetic sounds. His 1822 letter to the French Academy
      laying out this breakthrough effectively founded the modern field of Egyptology.
  - id: "2026-10-04-2"
    question: "Besides Ancient Greek and Egyptian hieroglyphs, what everyday cursive Egyptian script also appears on the Rosetta Stone, giving scholars a third version of the same decree to compare?"
    answer: "Demotic"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      Demotic was the fast, simplified script ordinary Egyptians used for daily business and legal
      documents, distinct from the formal, picture-based hieroglyphs reserved for temples and
      monuments. Having the same 196 BC decree written out in Greek, Demotic, and hieroglyphs on one
      slab meant scholars could use the readable Greek as a key to work backward into the other two.
      That side-by-side repetition is the entire reason the stone became the breakthrough it did.
  - id: "2026-10-04-3"
    question: "Soldiers fortifying a fort during which French general's 1799 Egyptian military campaign stumbled on the Rosetta Stone near the Nile Delta town of Rashid?"
    answer: "Napoleon Bonaparte"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      French troops rebuilding a decrepit fort near the town the French called Rosetta (Rashid in
      Arabic) found the inscribed granodiorite slab built into an old wall. After Napoleon's forces
      were later defeated in Egypt, the stone passed to the British under the terms of surrender and
      has been on display in the British Museum since 1802, where it remains one of its most visited
      objects.
