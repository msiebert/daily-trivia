  - id: "2026-10-06-1"
    question: "Which of the three waterfalls making up Niagara Falls carries about 90 percent of the Niagara River's flow and sits mostly on the Canadian side of the border?"
    answer: "Horseshoe Falls"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Niagara Falls is really a group of three waterfalls straddling the
      US-Canada border: Horseshoe Falls, American Falls, and the much
      smaller Bridal Veil Falls. Horseshoe Falls is by far the biggest
      and most powerful, curving roughly 2,600 feet across and funneling
      about 90 percent of the Niagara River's water over its edge. Its
      combined width and volume give it the highest flow rate of any
      waterfall on Earth with a drop over 50 meters. The much narrower
      American and Bridal Veil Falls, on the U.S. side, share the
      remaining tenth of the flow.
  - id: "2026-10-06-2"
    question: "What river, formed as the last ice age's glaciers retreated roughly 12,500 years ago, carries water from Lake Erie down to Lake Ontario and tumbles over Niagara Falls along the way?"
    answer: "Niagara River"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      As the ice sheets of the last glaciation pulled back from the
      Niagara Peninsula around 12,500 years ago, meltwater draining from
      the newly formed Great Lakes carved a short, steep channel linking
      Lake Erie to Lake Ontario. That channel is the Niagara River, and
      the falls themselves are simply the point where it drops over a
      resistant limestone ledge partway along its roughly 35-mile run.
      Because it connects two Great Lakes directly, it also carries an
      enormous volume of water for such a short river.
  - id: "2026-10-06-3"
    question: "Before 20th-century hydroelectric diversions and a more erosion-resistant caprock slowed it down, Niagara Falls' Horseshoe section was retreating upstream at an average rate of about how many feet per year?"
    answer: "3"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      For centuries, the rushing water undercut the softer shale beneath
      Horseshoe Falls' harder dolostone caprock, causing chunks of rock
      to collapse and the falls to creep upstream at an average of about
      three feet a year. Since large hydroelectric projects began
      diverting much of the Niagara River's flow in the mid-20th century,
      that retreat has slowed to roughly one foot a year. The falls have
      migrated more than 6 miles upstream over the roughly 12,500 years
      since they formed, carving out the Niagara Gorge behind them as
      they went.
